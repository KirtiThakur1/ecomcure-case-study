Decision 02 =E2=80=94 Checkout friction reduction<= /h3>

Cart abandonment is the most expensive UX problem i= n e-commerce. Every unnecessary step, every surprise, every moment of doubt= at checkout is a lost sale. The interventions here were almost always stru= ctural rather than visual.

Key principle

Shopify's analytics showed the exact step where users left the che= ckout. The payment page had a higher drop-off rate than the shippin= g page on almost every store. The cause was consistent: no visible= trust signals (SSL badge, accepted payment methods, return policy) at the = moment users were asked to enter card details. Adding these in the right pl= ace =E2=80=94 not at the top of the page, but adjacent to the payment form = =E2=80=94 reduced drop-off measurably.

This is the section that connects the past work to = where I am now. Studying HCI at Bauhaus-Universit=C3=A4t Weimar has given m= e a framework to look back at three years of commercial design work and see= clearly what was missing =E2=80=94 and what was actually stronger than I r= ealized at the time.

What I did then

Analytics as the only research

I used Shopify analytics and heatmaps as my primary =E2=80=94 of= ten only =E2=80=94 source of user insight. I knew where users dropped off. = I didn't know why.

What I'd add now

Even 3 user interviews would have changed everything

Three 20-minute interviews with actual customers would have surf= aced the reasoning behind the drop-off =E2=80=94 information no heatmap can= give you. The analytics told me what. Users tell you why.

What I did then

Redesigned based on best practices

I applied standard e-commerce conversion patterns =E2=80=94 abov= e-the-fold CTAs, trust badges, urgency signals =E2=80=94 without testing wh= ether they were right for that specific audience.

What I'd add now

Hypothesis-driven A/B testing

Each change should have been framed as a hypothesis with a measu= rable outcome. Not "add a trust badge" but "adding a trust badge adjacent t= o the payment form will reduce payment-step drop-off by X%." The discipline= of the hypothesis makes the learning transferable.

What I did then

Designed for the client's approval

Too often, design decisions were influenced by what the client f= ound visually appealing rather than what the data suggested. Client happine= ss and user success were sometimes in conflict =E2=80=94 and I didn't alway= s resolve that conflict correctly.

What I'd add now

Data as the shared language

The times I did bring analytics into client conversations =E2=80= =94 showing them specifically where users were dropping off =E2=80=94 the d= esign decisions became much less subjective. Data is the most effective too= l for removing personal preference from design discussions.

What this experience tau= ght me that HCI courses can't

  • 01

    Design decisions have real financial consequences

    In academic projects, a wrong design choice is a learning oppo= rtunity. In e-commerce, it's lost revenue. That accountability sharpens you= r thinking in a way that's hard to replicate in coursework.

  • 02

    Speed and iteration matter as much as correctness

    Shipping an imperfect improvement and measuring it beats spend= ing three weeks perfecting something untested. I learned this from necessit= y =E2=80=94 clients expected results within weeks, not months.

  • 03

    The user's mental model and the client's vision are often dif= ferent things

    Clients know their product deeply. They don't always know how = a first-time visitor experiences it. Bridging that gap =E2=80=94 diplomatic= ally, using data =E2=80=94 is the most commercially valuable UX skill I dev= eloped.

  • 04

    Behavioral email design is UX design

    A Klaviyo flow is a user journey. The timing, sequence, and to= ne of each email reflects a model of user behavior and emotional state. Des= igning it well requires exactly the same empathy and logic as designing a p= roduct screen.
