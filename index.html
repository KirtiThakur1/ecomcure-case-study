01 Context

15 STORES.

ONE LESSON.

EcomCure was a small digital agency building and optimizing Shopify stores for international e-commerce clients. My role was part designer, part analyst, part problem-solver — and entirely responsible for whether the store made money or just looked good.

Over nearly three years, I worked across 15+ client stores spanning fashion, beauty, homeware, and supplements. No two were the same problem. But the underlying tension was always identical: the gap between what a client thought their customers wanted and what the analytics actually showed.

01

Cart abandonment at checkout

Users would add products and disappear at the payment step. Usually caused by surprise shipping costs, too many form fields, or lack of trust signals at the critical moment.

02

Product page drop-off

High traffic to product pages, low add-to-cart rates. The page wasn't answering the user's actual questions — size information buried, social proof missing, CTAs competing with each other.

03

Homepage that confused instead of converted

Clients wanted to show everything. Users needed to understand one thing immediately. Every element added "just in case" was friction added to every user's journey.

04

Email campaigns with no behavioral logic

Sending the same email to every subscriber, regardless of what they'd browsed, purchased, or abandoned. High unsubscribe rates. Low revenue from the channel.

03 Design Decisions

WHAT I
CHANGED
AND WHY.

Each intervention started from the same place: the analytics. Shopify's built-in reporting showed funnel drop-off points. Heatmaps showed where users stopped scrolling or clicked on non-clickable elements. The design decisions followed from the data — not from aesthetic preference.

Decision 01 — Rebuilding the product page hierarchy

On most underperforming product pages, the visual hierarchy was wrong. The product name was large. The price was prominent. But the question the user actually needed answered first — "will this fit / work / suit me?" — was buried in a tab at the bottom.

After

Redesigned structure

Decision 02 — Checkout friction reduction

Cart abandonment is the most expensive UX problem in e-commerce. Every unnecessary step, every surprise, every moment of doubt at checkout is a lost sale. The interventions here were almost always structural rather than visual.

Key principle

Shopify's analytics showed the exact step where users left the checkout. The payment page had a higher drop-off rate than the shipping page on almost every store. The cause was consistent: no visible trust signals (SSL badge, accepted payment methods, return policy) at the moment users were asked to enter card details. Adding these in the right place — not at the top of the page, but adjacent to the payment form — reduced drop-off measurably.

Decision 03 — Email segmentation in Klaviyo

The biggest leverage point in email marketing is not better design — it's better targeting. Sending a "buy now" email to someone who bought yesterday is noise. Sending it to someone who viewed the product three times but never purchased is revenue.

Broadcast approach (before)
Behavioral flows (after)

One list, one message

  • ✕Same email to all subscribers regardless of behavior
  • ✕Promotional focus — always pushing to buy
  • ✕No timing logic — sent on arbitrary schedule
  • ✕High unsubscribe rates from irrelevant content

Segmented behavioral flows

  • ✓Welcome series for new subscribers — education first, sell second
  • ✓Cart abandonment flow — triggered 1hr, 24hr, 72hr after abandonment
  • ✓Post-purchase flow — review request + cross-sell at right timing
  • ✓Win-back flow for subscribers inactive 90+ days

05 Reflection

WHAT I'D
DO DIFFERENTLY
NOW.

This is the section that connects the past work to where I am now. Studying HCI at Bauhaus-Universität Weimar has given me a framework to look back at three years of commercial design work and see clearly what was missing — and what was actually stronger than I realized at the time.

What I did then

Analytics as the only research

I used Shopify analytics and heatmaps as my primary — often only — source of user insight. I knew where users dropped off. I didn't know why.

What I'd add now

Even 3 user interviews would have changed everything

Three 20-minute interviews with actual customers would have surfaced the reasoning behind the drop-off — information no heatmap can give you. The analytics told me what. Users tell you why.

What I did then

Redesigned based on best practices

I applied standard e-commerce conversion patterns — above-the-fold CTAs, trust badges, urgency signals — without testing whether they were right for that specific audience.

What I'd add now

Hypothesis-driven A/B testing

Each change should have been framed as a hypothesis with a measurable outcome. Not "add a trust badge" but "adding a trust badge adjacent to the payment form will reduce payment-step drop-off by X%." The discipline of the hypothesis makes the learning transferable.

What I did then

Designed for the client's approval

Too often, design decisions were influenced by what the client found visually appealing rather than what the data suggested. Client happiness and user success were sometimes in conflict — and I didn't always resolve that conflict correctly.

What I'd add now

Data as the shared language

The times I did bring analytics into client conversations — showing them specifically where users were dropping off — the design decisions became much less subjective. Data is the most effective tool for removing personal preference from design discussions.

What this experience taught me that HCI courses can't

  • 01

    Design decisions have real financial consequences

    In academic projects, a wrong design choice is a learning opportunity. In e-commerce, it's lost revenue. That accountability sharpens your thinking in a way that's hard to replicate in coursework.

  • 02

    Speed and iteration matter as much as correctness

    Shipping an imperfect improvement and measuring it beats spending three weeks perfecting something untested. I learned this from necessity — clients expected results within weeks, not months.

  • 03

    The user's mental model and the client's vision are often different things

    Clients know their product deeply. They don't always know how a first-time visitor experiences it. Bridging that gap — diplomatically, using data — is the most commercially valuable UX skill I developed.

  • 04

    Behavioral email design is UX design

    A Klaviyo flow is a user journey. The timing, sequence, and tone of each email reflects a model of user behavior and emotional state. Designing it well requires exactly the same empathy and logic as designing a product screen.
